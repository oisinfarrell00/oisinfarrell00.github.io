# Introduction

Carol Dweck is a Stanford psychologist who spent decades studying why some people thrive under challenge while others crumble. _Mindset_ distils that research into one elegantly simple idea: the beliefs we hold about our own abilities shape almost everything — how we handle failure, how we approach learning, and ultimately how far we go. It sounds almost too simple. It isn't.

# The Core Idea

The book draws a distinction between two ways of seeing yourself. A fixed mindset holds that your qualities — intelligence, talent, character — are essentially set. You either have it or you don't. A growth mindset believes these qualities can be developed through effort and experience. Dweck builds the case across research, sport, business, and education with enough real-world grounding that the ideas never feel abstract or airy.

What separates _Mindset_ from the usual self-help fare is that it actually worked on me. I have read plenty of books in this genre that leave you energised for a week before the lessons quietly dissolve into the background noise of daily life. This one was different. I found myself genuinely digesting the ideas and, more importantly, implementing them. I would be doing a hard task and I would randomly catch myself reminding myself to enjoy the hard task, this will make things better in the long run. That kind of genuine behavioural shift is rare for a book, at least for me.

# A Note on Relevance

Not every chapter will land equally for every reader, and the book is structured in a way that lets you take what is useful and leave the rest. There is a substantial section aimed at parents on raising children with a growth mindset. As someone without children, I skipped it without guilt. What remained was more than enough.
