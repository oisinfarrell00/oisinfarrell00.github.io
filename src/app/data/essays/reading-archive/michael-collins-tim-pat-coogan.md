# Introduction

In the crowded landscape of Irish revolutionary history, few figures loom as large or as polarising as Michael Collins. In this definitive biography, Tim Pat Coogan provides an exhaustive and often exhilarating account of the man known as "The Big Fellow." Coogan doesn't just chronicle a life; he maps the birth of a nation through the lens of its most pragmatic and tireless architect.

# The Man Behind the Myth

The book reveals a complex character: a man who was simultaneously a ruthless director of intelligence, a meticulous bean-counter for the National Loan, and a boisterous friend prone to play-fighting. We see Collins not as a remote general, but as a "bureaucrat guerrilla" who understood that wars are won as much by logistics and information as they are by bullets.

# The Architecture of Resistance

The "bones" of the narrative focus on Collins' mastery of the shadows. Coogan detail's Collins’ development of the "Squad" and his vast network of informants—from G-men in Dublin Castle to postal workers and railway clerks. The book brilliantly illustrates how Collins inverted the traditional rules of engagement, turning the British administration’s own bureaucracy against itself.

# The Great Schism

A significant portion of the work is dedicated to the tragic friction between Collins and Éamon de Valera. Coogan is notably sympathetic to Collins, portraying him as the realist who accepted the "stepping stone" of the Anglo-Irish Treaty, while framing de Valera’s opposition as a mixture of ideological purity and personal ego. This tension serves as the book's emotional core, driving the narrative toward its inevitable, somber conclusion in the Irish Civil War. The book definitely paints Collins to be the good guy, while de Valera let Collins travel to England to 'sign his actual dead warrent' as he put it. Wether Collins did the right thing or not is a topic to explore another time.

# The Legacy of Béal na Bláth

The final chapters are a poignant reflection on "what might have been." Coogan captures the chaos and the profound sense of loss surrounding the ambush at Béal na Bláth. By the end of the biography, the reader is left with the impression of a man who burned out with incredible intensity, leaving behind a functioning—if fractured—state.

# Final Verdict

Tim Pat Coogan’s Michael Collins is a masterful, if occasionally biased, tribute to a singular historical force. While modern revisionist historians might argue with some of Coogan’s more hagiographic tendencies, the book’s depth of research and narrative pace make it a gripping read. It is a vital portrait of a man who possessed the rare combination of physical courage and administrative genius required to change the course of history. My only complaint of this book, if there had to be one, is the hagiographic telling of Michael Collins' life. It talks exclusively positively about him.

# Rating

9
