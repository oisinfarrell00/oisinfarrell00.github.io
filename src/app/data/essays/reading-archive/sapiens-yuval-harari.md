# Introduction

Yuval Noah Harari set out to do something almost fairly ambitious with _Sapiens_: to summarise the entire history of humanity in a single book. In my opinion, he very nearly pulls it off. The book sweeps from the Cognitive Revolution 70,000 years ago all the way through to the modern world, and Harari handles this in a way that is digestible and easy to follow.

# The Grand Sweep

Harari's great gift is for the high-altitude view. He is not interested in the granular details of any particular century or civilisation — he is always asking the bigger questions. Why did one species of primate come to dominate the planet? What role did shared myths, collective belief, and language play in the rise of cultures, empires, and religions? How did _Homo sapiens_ manage to cooperate in the enormous numbers required to build civilisations in the first place?

The early sections of the book are extraordinary. The chapters dealing with prehistoric humans, the Agricultural Revolution, and the emergence of the first complex societies are among the most fascinating passages I have read in any non-fiction work. Harari makes you feel as though you are seeing human history — the whole sweeping arc of it — clearly for the first time.

# Where It Dips

My enthusiasm did wane as the book moved into modern history. The sections covering the Industrial Revolution, capitalism, and the contemporary world felt less captivaing. Perhaps the material is more familiar, or perhaps the high level approach that gives the book a nice pace to begin with loses some of its magic with modern history. Or maybe I was simply more interested in the early history of humans. Either way, in my opinion, the early chapters set such a high bar that the later ones struggle to match it.

That is a minor complaint in the context of the whole. As an overview of the total human story, _Sapiens_ more than earns its reputation.

# Rating

7
