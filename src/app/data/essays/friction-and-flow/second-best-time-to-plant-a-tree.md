# Introduction

The best time to plant a tree? Twenty years ago. The second best time? Now. I love this saying. It applies to almost anything — picking up a new sport as an adult, changing careers, finally starting to invest. Whatever it is, there's usually an initial hesitation, a little voice saying _it's too late, you should have started years ago_. And that voice isn't wrong, exactly. If only you had started earlier. But the fact of the matter is you didn't, and that doesn't mean you can't start now. The best time to plant that tree was twenty years ago. The second best time is today.

---

# The Maths of Compounding

Money, skills, and habits all compound over time. You've probably heard the version of this about investing €100 a week into an index fund for 40 years and ending up a millionaire — and it's true, time is doing most of the heavy lifting in that number. The earlier you start, the more time compounding has to work, and the bigger the eventual gap between "started early" and "started late" becomes.

But here's the part that gets lost when people only focus on the 40-year graph: compounding doesn't switch off just because you missed the first ten or twenty years of it. It just means you're compounding from a later starting point instead of no starting point at all. Money, skills, and habits all still grow from here — the curve is just shifted along the timeline, not erased.

---

# "Too Late" Is a Trap, Not a Fact

Charlie Munger was once asked, in front of a room full of people at the 2019 Daily Journal Annual Meeting, why his long-time business partner Warren Buffett was so much richer than he was. Munger's answer was simple: "He got an earlier start." At the time, Buffett was worth somewhere north of $80 billion. Munger was worth about $1.6 billion.

It's a great illustration of time in the market beating timing the market — except the story usually gets told with an important detail missing. By the time Munger passed away in 2023, his net worth was still over $2 billion. That's not "started too late and it didn't matter." That's "started a bit later than the best possible time, and still built an extraordinary fortune." Buffett ending up richer than Munger doesn't mean Munger's second-best start was a failure. If Munger had talked himself out of investing at all because he'd missed his own twenty-years-ago moment, there'd be no $2 billion to talk about.

This is where present bias quietly does its damage. Economists O'Donoghue and Rabin (1999) modelled exactly this pattern: people with present-biased preferences consistently put off costly-but-worthwhile actions, always assuming their future self will simply get around to it. "I've already missed the best time, I'll sort it out later" is present bias wearing a very convincing disguise — it feels like a reasonable conclusion about timing, but it's really just procrastination borrowing the tree metaphor to justify itself.

---

# Regret

If the maths doesn't convince you, the psychology of regret might. Gilovich and Medvec's classic research on the "temporal pattern of regret" (1994) found something that maps almost perfectly onto this saying: in the short term, people tend to regret the things they _did_ more than the things they didn't. But zoom out to the long term, and it flips entirely — the regrets that linger for years, the ones people bring up when asked about their biggest life regrets, are overwhelmingly regrets of inaction, not action.

There's a reason for this. Gilovich and Medvec found that regretted actions fade faster because you can actually do something about them — apologise, course-correct, learn the lesson and move on. Regretted inactions don't offer that same closure. There's no "fixing" a tree you never planted twenty years ago; there's only the quiet, open-ended wondering about what might have grown by now. That's the version of regret that doesn't shrink with time. It just sits there.

So the choice was never really "start twenty years ago or don't bother." It's "accept a short-term regret now (the discomfort of starting late, of being a beginner at your age) in exchange for avoiding a much longer-lasting one later (never having started at all)."

---

# Takeaway

No matter how late you think you are to the party, starting now will always beat not starting at all. The regret of a late beginning fades. The regret of no beginning doesn't. Twenty years ago is gone. Today isn't.
