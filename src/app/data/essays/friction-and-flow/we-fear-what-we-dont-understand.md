# Introduction

There have been many instances where I've been fearful of something right up until the moment I understood it. The most recent, and I doubt I'm alone here, was the AI boom. The consensus a couple of years ago was that AI was going to take over — steal all our jobs, make us obsolete. I won't pretend none of that is happening to some degree. But my initial reaction, that gut-punch feeling of _it's over for me_, was mostly fear, not reality.

Avoiding AI was never really an option. Jobs started expecting you to use it, so the thing I was afraid of had to be explored whether I liked it or not. As time passed and I actually started using it, I realised it's an extremely useful tool. If AI does eventually replace me, at least it made my final months a lot more productive. Work I never had the bandwidth, time, or knowledge for suddenly became possible. I didn't fear AI. I just didn't understand it, or know how to make it work for me.

This post isn't really about AI, though. It's about a pattern I keep running into: the things I've been most afraid of are, almost without exception, the things I understood least.

---

# The Pattern

The clearest, most universal example of this might be driving. Nearly everyone is nervous before they start. The gears, the rules of the road, merging lanes — it's high-pressure, and to top it off, serious accidents genuinely can happen. And yet for most people, driving eventually becomes automatic. You stop having to think about it. Any stress that's left tends to be about an unfamiliar road or a confusing exit, not the actual mechanics of operating the car. That part becomes second nature.

There's a pattern to how this unfolds, and it repeats almost identically every time:

1. **You don't understand something.** You're hesitant to try it, maybe you avoid it outright.
2. **You're forced to face it.** Sometimes it's mandatory — AI at work — sometimes it's just something you've decided you want, like driving.
3. **It's hard at first.** The car stalls. The AI output is mediocre and doesn't save you any time.
4. **You keep going anyway**, and bit by bit you get more efficient, more fluent, more confident.
5. **It becomes easy. Then frictionless. Then mundane** — and you look back and barely recognise the person who was afraid of it.

---

# It's a Knowledge Gap

People often assume investing is "for rich people," or "for people who get that sort of thing." Almost always, it's just a knowledge gap wearing a disguise. We assume we _can't_, because we don't have a starting point, no prior experience to lean on. The thing is a black box, and our brains don't like black boxes. We fear what we don't understand, so we avoid it — which, conveniently, guarantees we never understand it.

This isn't just a hunch. Van Rooij, Lusardi, and Alessie (2011) surveyed thousands of households and found that financial literacy predicts stock market participation _independently of income_ — it isn't just wealthy people who invest, it's people who understand roughly how investing works, regardless of how much they have to invest. The knowledge gap, not the wealth gap, is doing a lot of the work here.

The way through it is simple to say and slower to do: chip away at the knowledge gap. Remind yourself that every expert started out exactly where you are now, and that understanding, much like a stock position, compounds. The more you learn, the more willing you become to keep learning — a nice little feedback loop, if you can get it started.

# We Don't Understand What We Fear

The same idea works in reverse, and this is the part I find genuinely unsettling: we don't just fail to understand things that scare us — we tend to _overestimate_ how well we understand the things that scare us in the first place.

Psychologists call this the **illusion of explanatory depth** (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002). People feel they understand complex systems — how an economy works, how a nuclear plant works, how an algorithm makes a decision — with far more precision and depth than they actually do. The gap in understanding sits there quietly, unnoticed, until something forces it into view. Usually, that something is a headline.

You can see this play out with real consequences, not just abstract cognitive bias. Fernbach and colleagues (2019) found that the people most strongly opposed to genetically modified foods knew the least about the science, while believing they knew the most. The fear and the confidence were both inversely related to actual understanding.

Nuclear power is maybe the cleanest example of this gap between perceived and actual risk. Slovic's classic research on risk perception (1987) found that nuclear power consistently scores as one of the most _dreaded_ risks in the public imagination — right up there with nuclear war — driven largely by two factors: how unfamiliar and how uncontrollable it feels, rather than by how many people it actually harms. It's a hazard most people have never had to understand in any real depth, so it gets filed under "catastrophic" by default, regardless of the underlying statistics.

And this isn't a new phenomenon that arrived with the internet — it's just been supercharged by it. When the steam locomotive was introduced in the 1800s, serious commentators worried that the human body wasn't built to withstand such speeds, and that the sensation of rail travel could actually cause insanity — the so-called "railway madness" panic. The telegraph drew accusations that it would flatten the English language into disconnected snippets. In 1941, a study in the _Journal of Pediatrics_ concluded that more than half of children were dangerously "addicted" to crime dramas on the radio. Every single one of these fears was really the same fear wearing a different costume: something new arrived, nobody had a mental model for it yet, and dread rushed in to fill the space where understanding should have been. AI is just the latest costume.

To make matters worse, social media doesn't create this fear, it just amplifies it. Platforms are optimised to promote emotionally charged content, which gives a disproportionate megaphone to the loudest, most fearful minority. And people are, in general, more likely to act on negative emotion than positive — a scary claim travels further and faster than a reassuring one. Facebook isn't the root cause of the fear. It's just a very efficient delivery mechanism for a knowledge gap that was already there.

---

# Takeaway

Whether you're learning a new skill or forming an opinion about something you've never actually looked into, the same principle applies: seek to understand _before_ you decide how you feel. Fear, more often than not, is just the sensation of an unfilled mental model. It feels like danger, but it's usually just a gap.

The good news is that gaps can be closed. The AI that once felt like a threat is now a tool I reach for daily. The car that once felt terrifying is now something I barely think about. The only real difference between the fear and the comfort was understanding — and understanding was always available to me, I just had to go and get it.
