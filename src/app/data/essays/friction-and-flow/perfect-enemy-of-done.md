# Introduction

We have all been there: a brilliant idea hits you, and in that exact moment, everything feels inevitable. Maybe it’s a personal goal, a business idea you know could make millions, or a determination to finally get fit. You begin to picture the reality of your success, almost tasting the achievement before you’ve even started.

But then, you never actually reach the finish line. Why?

I have been trapped in this loop more times than I can count. I get a great idea, map out a rough plan in my head, and sit down to build it. Then, a sudden shift happens. A voice creeps in: _"Oh, I could add this feature. Wouldn't it be incredible if it also did XYZ?"_

Suddenly, the scope balloons. The initial dopamine hit from the brainstorming phase evaporates, and the grind of making actual progress begins. Every time you finally cross something off, you spot three more potential features. You add them to the to-do list. Even though you long surpassed your original vision, you are still never quite "done."

---

# Perfect is the Enemy of Done

There will always be more you can do. Always. In a way, that’s a good thing — having a backlog keeps you hungry. But if perfection is your ultimate benchmark, you can kiss "done" goodbye.

Perfection is an illusion. It's a moving target. While ambition is necessary to keep us motivated, it is equally important to step back, look at what you've built, and say: _"This is good enough. This is done."_

If you constantly hold off on shipping until the next feature is ready, someone else with a far worse product will beat you to market. They will launch a stripped-back version of your idea, but they will capture the users and the traction. Why? Because they had the guts to ship. A project sitting in development or planning does no good to anyone.

I’m not judging if you find yourself here, nor do I claim to have the magic cure. I still battle this exact mindset to this day. You are not alone. However, I am actively trying to get better at executing, and here are the guardrails I use to keep myself on track.

---

# Strategies

## 1. Define Your MVP Early

Decide at the very beginning what your Minimum Viable Product (MVP) looks like. What is the bare minimum criteria this project needs to meet to be considered functional and complete?

Write that down and guard it with your life. Every time a new feature idea or "nice-to-have" pops into your head, write it down on a separate list. **None of those items get touched until the MVP is fully shipped.** Non-negotiable.

## 2. Just Start

Once your MVP is defined or your idea is clear, just start. The conditions aren't perfect? Start anyway. Waiting for the right moment? Start anyway. Even if you aren't entirely sure what the first step looks like, do _something_.

Making a mistake is infinitely better than standing still. The old Irish proverb says _"a good start is half the battle,"_ but any start is better than none. You will figure it out along the way. The perfect starting point doesn't exist, and it's irrelevant anyway. Write that first line of code, open that empty file, and see where it takes you.

## 3. Commit Small and Follow Through

One of my biggest hurdles is an "all-or-nothing" mindset. If I can't commit to the gym seven days a week, my brain tells me to go zero days. It’s a funny piece of human behavior, but we see it all the time. Someone starts a new routine, pumps out intense, two-hour sessions for a few weeks, and inevitably burns out or loses interest. One missed day spirals into never going back.

The antidote is to commit to the smallest, most consistent unit of progress possible. If you plan to run a marathon, start by running a light 5k every two days consistently. I have met so many people who go to the gym for 1-2 hours a day. They scoff at the idea of going for just 30 minutes daily. Then they inevitably stop going. I personally hate the gym, but I know its good for me so I go 30 minutes 3/4 times a week. I can do this week after week. I dont dread it because its only 30 minutes and I know I only have to make up 3-4 sessions to feel like Ive checked it off and now I have been attending the gym for years.

When you operate in extremes, perfection easily eliminates progress. Breaking things down into small, non-threatening increments is the only way to keep "perfect" from killing "done."

## 4. Let Feedback Find Your Features

When you are deep in development, you have total tunnel vision. You breeze through user flows because you designed them. You avoid errors because you already know where the edge cases hide.

You cannot accurately predict how a real user will interact with your system—what buttons they’ll press, what inputs they’ll try, or most importantly, what they actually want. Stop guessing. Get the product into human hands as early as possible and let actual usage dictate your roadmap.

## 5. Reshape Your Mindset

If you’re wired like me, this is the hardest part. The best way to beat perfectionism is to fundamentally reframe how you value execution.

We all instinctively want our work to be flawless. But the reality is that no one expects your first version to be perfect, and everyone agrees that a launched, working project is infinitely better than a perfect one that only exists on your local machine.
