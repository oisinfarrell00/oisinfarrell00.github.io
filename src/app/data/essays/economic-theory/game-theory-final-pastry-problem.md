# Brightflag's Pastry Dilemma

![TEST](/data/economic-theory/images/image2.png)

In work pastries arrive early in the morning when people are just starting to shuffle in. Perfect timing to pair with that morning coffee. There are always a variety of pastries. One may find themselves in the following predicament: they go to get a pastry with a colleage. There is only one left. Both people see it, both people want it. Yet, both people hestitate to move. This situation plays out in one of 4 ways:

- One Person moves while the other hesitates. One winner, one looser!
- Neither move. Both walk away. The pastry goes stale. What a waste.
- Both people reach, there hands awkwardly meeting as they the scramble to retract. Awkward!

Heres where things get interesting. The best 'strategy' for getting the pastry is always to grab. See, if you grab and they dont, then youre the big winner! But even if they gran you should too. Its better to try and fail than not try at all. Grabbing each time doesnt always guarantee success, but it guarantees you dont _loose_. So, rationally you should always grab. But if both players always act rationally, then we would only ever have an awkward situation and nobody would ever get the pastry. And this my friend, is the prisoners dilemma.

# The Prisoners Dilemma

![TEST](/data/economic-theory/images/image3.png)

The prisoners dilemma is part of a broader topic in economics known as Game Theory which aims to understand how people make decisions when other peoples decisions matter.

The original prisoners dilemma goes as follows:
Two theives are caught and immediately seperated so they cant communciate. Then each is given a choice. They can either snitch or honor the thief's code and remain silent. The number of years each thief recieves is determined not only by their choice, but also the choice of the other theif.

- Remaining silent while the other snitches carries the maximum penalty.
- if both theives snitch then they will both go to prison for 2 years.
- if both theives remain silent they will both go to prison for only two years.

Again we see the same pattern. The rational thing to do is always snitch. Snitching means you might walk away scot free in the best case (if the other remaing silent), and at the very least you avoid the maximum penalty. However, if both thieves always act rationally and snitch they will always get the collective worst outcome when the collective best (only 1 year) could have been achieved.

# The Prisoners Dilemma: The Cold War

![TEST](/data/economic-theory/images/image4.png)

The Cold War was an example of the Prisoners Dilemma played out in real life. Like the other examples one can see a pattern emerging. The 'rational' choice, in this case to arm yourself with nuclear weapons, leads to a collecitvely worse outcome. See, if you had the choice to arm yourself with nuclear weapons you should. This means you are either the dominant force, or at the very least you have some defense againt the other. However, if everyone acts rationally we again miss the collective best outcome which is for nobody to have nuclear weapons. Sometimes individual rationale is at the expense of the collective.

# How to win

![TEST](/data/economic-theory/images/image5.png)

In the 80s professor by the name of Robert Axelrod set out to determine what is the best strategy for winning a rerpeated prisobers deilemma, ie a real world prisoners dilemma. To do this he ran a tournament where people could submit strategies that would each play eachother in a random number of iterations wherein each strategy could split or steal a number of gold coins.

A full video breakdown can be found [here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mScpHTIi-kM&t=2s&pp=ygUdZ2FtZSB0aGVvcnkgdGF0aWNzIHNpbXVsYXRpb24%3D). Axelrod discovered that while there was no perfet strategy for winning there were 4 key characteristics of a winning formula:

1. Be kind: Dont steal first
2. Be forgiving: Allow the harmony to return to splitting after the other has defected.
3. Be retaliatory: Don't allow your opponent to steal without stealing back!
4. Be clear: A chaotic strategy cannot be trusted
